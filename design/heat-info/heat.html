What the heat index means

What the number means, what the heat can do to your body, and what to do about it.

This is general advice, not official DepEd or PAGASA guidance. If someone is very unwell, tell a teacher and get medical help.

What is the heat index?

It is how hot it feels to your body. It uses the air temperature and the humidity together.

When the air is humid, sweat dries slowly, so your body cannot cool down well. That is why 33 °C with high humidity feels much hotter than 33 °C in dry air.

The levels and numbers below are from PAGASA. This device measures inside the classroom.

Caution

Heat index 27 to 32 °C

What it can do to the body

You can get tired if you stay active in the heat for a long time.

What to prepare and do

  • Keep a water bottle with you.
  • If you feel tired, rest in a shady or cooler place.

Extreme caution

Heat index 33 to 41 °C

What it can do to the body

Heat cramps (painful tight muscles) and heat exhaustion (very tired, dizzy, sweating a lot) can happen. If you keep going without a rest, it can get worse.

What to prepare and do

  • Drink water often, even if you are not thirsty.
  • Wear light-coloured, loose clothes.
  • Open the windows and use a fan if you have one.
  • Take rest breaks in the shade.
  • Do hard activities, like sports, in a cooler place or at a cooler time.

Danger

Heat index 42 to 51 °C

What it can do to the body

Heat cramps and heat exhaustion are likely. Heat stroke can happen if you stay active in this heat.

What to prepare and do

  • Cut down or stop hard activity, most of all between 12:00 and 4:00 in the afternoon.
  • Move class activities to the coolest, shadiest place you have.
  • Drink water often and rest often.
  • Tell a teacher right away if someone feels unwell.
  • Learn the warning signs below.

Extreme danger

Heat index 52 °C and up

What it can do to the body

Heat stroke is very likely. Heat stroke is an emergency.

What to prepare and do

  • Stay out of the heat. No hard activity.
  • Move everyone to the coolest place.
  • Drink water.
  • If someone shows a warning sign, tell a teacher and get medical help straight away.

Warning signs

Heat exhaustion

  • Heavy sweating, cool and damp skin
  • Feeling dizzy, weak or very tired
  • Headache or feeling sick

What to do: tell a teacher. Move to a cooler, shady place. Loosen tight clothes. Sip water.

Heat stroke

  • Confusion, or not making sense
  • Fainting
  • Very hot skin, with or without sweat

What to do: this is an emergency. Get an adult and medical help straight away. Move the person to a cooler place while you wait.

When you are not sure, treat it as serious and ask for help.
